# Timeline of chat and instant messaging

## Pre‑internet and early networked messaging (1960s–1970s)

1961–1965: MIT’s Compatible Time‑Sharing System (CTSS) allows logged‑in users to send short text messages to each other on a shared mainframe; by 1965 it has hundreds of users at MIT, exhibiting modern IM‑like behavior.

Early 1970s: IM‑style chat functions appear on government and research networks, establishing the pattern of real‑time text exchange between users on a shared system.

## First internet‑wide protocols and chat rooms (1980s–early 1990s)

1980: CB Simulator on CompuServe offers one of the first commercial “chat room” experiences.

1985: Quantum Link (Q‑Link) for Commodore machines later becomes part of what will be known as America Online (AOL).

1988–1989: Internet Relay Chat (IRC) is created and spreads globally; it becomes the internet’s first widespread instant messaging standard, based on channels (“chat rooms”) and client–server architecture.

Early 1990s: IRC peaks in popularity for group chat, especially in technical and hobbyist communities; many open‑source clients and servers appear.

## Consumer IM boom: closed platforms and buddy lists (mid‑1990s–early 2000s)

1994: PowWow launches as an early GUI‑based IM client.

1996: ICQ (Mirabilis, "I Seek You") launches and becomes the first widely adopted closed/commercialized IM service, introducing features like online presence, one‑to‑one chat, group chat, file transfer, and user search.

1997: AOL Instant Messenger (AIM) launches, popularizing the “buddy list” concept and dominating North American IM in the early 2000s.

1998: Yahoo! Messenger launches, integrating with Yahoo accounts and services.

1999:

- XMPP (Jabber) emerges as an open, federated IM protocol (standardized later by the IETF). Jabber ID like email address: user@domain.com.

- MSN Messenger launches as Microsoft’s consumer IM client.

- Emojis are first introduced in Japan, later becoming a global feature of chat apps.

Early 2000s: AIM, ICQ, Yahoo, and MSN battle for market share; Skype (2003) adds VoIP plus IM; Google Talk (2005) brings XMPP‑based chat into Gmail.

## Mobile messaging and chat apps overtake SMS (mid‑2000s–2010s)

2005: BlackBerry Messenger (BBM) becomes the first popular mobile‑centric IM, blending traditional IM with mobile SMS‑like behavior on BlackBerry devices.

Late 2000s: Social networks embed chat (e.g., Facebook Chat in 2008), blurring lines between social media and IM.

2009–2011: Smartphone‑native chat apps explode:

- WhatsApp (2009) offers free texting over data.

- Kik and Viber (2010).

- Facebook Messenger and Snapchat (2011).

2013: Chat apps globally surpass SMS in message volume.

2013–2015: New generations of chat platforms appear:

- Telegram (2013) focuses on speed and large groups.

- Signal (2014) emphasizes strong encryption and privacy.

- Discord (2015) starts as a low‑latency voice/text platform for gamers, then expands to broader communities.

## Enterprise/team chat and the “Slack era” (2010s)

2010: HipChat launches as an early team‑focused chat tool; later acquired by Atlassian (2012) and killed.

2013: Slack launches publicly and rapidly becomes the de facto standard for team chat in startups and tech companies.

2014–2016:

- Zulip is founded (2012), acquired by Dropbox (2014), open‑sourced (2015), then spun out under Kandra Labs (2016) with a unique topic‑threaded model.

- Mattermost and Rocket.Chat emerge as open‑source, self‑hostable Slack‑like platforms.

- Matrix protocol is created (2014); the reference client launches as Vector (2015), later rebranded Riot (2016), then Element (2019+), enabling decentralized, federated chat.

2018–2019: Atlassian discontinues HipChat and Stride, migrating users to Slack; HipChat’s IP is sold to Slack.

## Open, decentralized, and sovereign chat (late 2010s–2020s)

2016–2021: Element/Riot and Matrix gain traction for secure, federated communication, including in public sector and enterprise deployments.

2015–2026: Open‑source team chat matures:

- Mattermost, Rocket.Chat, Zulip, Nextcloud Talk, and Element (Tchap) become core options for self‑hosted, GDPR‑friendly, and sovereign deployments.

- Stoat (formerly Revolt) appears as an open‑source, user‑first chat platform, explicitly positioned as a self‑hosted Discord alternative; the project officially renames from Revolt to Stoat around 2025.

2020s: XMPP remains active with modern clients (Gajim, Conversations, Dino, Converse, Movim) implementing newer extensions (OMEMO encryption, reactions, replies, MAM, etc.), especially in communities valuing federation, openness, and privacy
