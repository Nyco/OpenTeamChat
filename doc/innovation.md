## Short list of chat/IM innovations across time

Here are key innovations that shaped instant messaging and chat, in roughly chronological order:

Multi‑user terminal-based messaging (1960s): First real‑time text exchange between users on shared mainframes, establishing the basic idea of IM.

Chat rooms (1980): Commercial “chat room” experiences like CompuServe’s CB Simulator, introducing persistent multi‑user text spaces.

Channels in IRC (1988–1989): IRC (Internet Relay Chat) adds global, channel‑based group chat with a client–server protocol, becoming the internet’s first widespread IM standard.

Buddy/contact lists (roser) and presence (mid‑1990s): ICQ (and AIM) popularize contact lists in a dedicated and separated window, with online/offline status and avatars, and one‑to‑one chat as a core consumer experience, with some kind of file transfer and typing notifications, and all sorts of annoying gadgets like buzz/nudge.

Open, federated protocol (1999): The open source community-based Jabber protocol defines an open, extensible, decentralised and federated IM and presence/status protocol, enabling multiple servers and clients to interoperate like email, later to be normalised and standardised by IETF.

VoIP and IM convergence (early 2000s): Skype and later Google Talk blend voice calls with instant messaging, foreshadowing unified communications.

Mobile‑first IM and SMS replacement (late 2000s–early 2010s): BBM, WhatsApp, and similar apps create a wave of smartphone-based IM, using data instead of SMS and adding push notifications, with core features as data persistence, read/delivered marks, group chats, replies/quotes, emojis, and inline media sharing (attachments: sound, images).

Chat surpassing SMS (2013): Global message volume in chat apps overtakes traditional SMS, marking the shift from telco‑centric to app‑centric messaging (OTT, "Over The Top" from the point of view of Telco), with many more attractive features as compared to SMS and limited evolutions like MMS.

Team Chat as a workflow hub (2013–2015): HipChat leads the innovation, Slack popularises it, and successors turn chat into a central collaboration layer with multi-user channels, threads, reactions, deep app integrations, search, and bots, both on synchronised mobile and desktop/laptop, breaking through enterprise and democratising IM along with email for the first time in history.

Privacy-focused messaging and modern E2EE (2013–2016): Signal and other privacy-orientated messengers make strong end-to-end encryption increasingly accessible, with cryptographic confidentiality becoming a central easy-to-use feature.

Topic‑threaded chat (2012+): Zulip introduces topic‑based threading inside channels, improving asynchronous collaboration and information architecture for teams.

Community servers with voice + text (2015+): Discord popularizes persistent “servers” with multiple text and low‑latency voice channels, optimized for communities and real‑time interaction.

Decentralized, sovereign chat (2014+): Matrix (with Element/Riot) follows the path of modern XMPP/Jabber ecosystems (Gajim, Conversations, Dino, Kaidan, Psi/Psi+), and promises a return of open and federated/decentralised architecture, with some E2EE encryption, as alternatives for privacy and sovereignty‑focused users and organizations. 

Self‑hosted, open‑source team chat (2010s–2020s): Mattermost, Rocket.Chat, Zulip, Nextcloud Talk, and Stoat/Revolt offer Slack/Discord‑like experiences that organizations can run themselves, addressing data control and compliance needs.
